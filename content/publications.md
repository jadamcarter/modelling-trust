## Forthcoming

### Trust Transport

Nicolas Côté, J. Adam Carter and Emma C. Gordon · *Ergo: An Open Access Journal of Philosophy*, forthcoming
{.paper-meta}

Experts often opine outside their home domain of expertise. A Nobel Prize-winning physicist might give television interviews, or even write a book, about religion. Or skilled actors might try to sell you a diet by citing health benefits. How much should we trust them? Philosophers writing on "epistemic trespassing" – roughly: speaking outside of one's area of expertise – agree that expertise in one field does not simply "carry over" to another, but nobody has said how much of it carries over, or why. This paper lays out an answer. As we'll argue, two things settle the matter, though each as we'll see is difficult to pin down: (i) how closely the two subject matters are related, and (ii) how broad a range of questions the expert has actually shown themselves to be reliable about. We attempt to make both ideas precise. Using David Lewis's account of subject matters, relatedness becomes a measurable quantity, namely how much settling every question in one field would tell you about the other, and breadth becomes the generality of the subject matter an expert's track record covers. From these we construct a rule for adjusting trust as an expert moves between fields, and prove that it satisfies four conditions any adequate account must meet, conditions that simpler proposals fail. We close by showing what the rule implies for epistemic trespassing and for how broken trust can be rebuilt.
